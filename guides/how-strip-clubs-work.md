# How strip clubs work

What the business is, who makes money and how, and why so many clubs end up in court.

## The industry in numbers

- About 3,900 strip club businesses operate in the U.S. IBISWorld estimated 3,889 in 2025, with about $4.2 billion in revenue.
- Revenue has been flat to falling. IBISWorld points to inflation squeezing customers' spending and to competition from online platforms.
- It's fragmented. Most clubs have local, independent owners. The largest public company, RCI Hospitality, ran about 59 adult clubs at the end of fiscal 2025 and reported $279.4 million in total revenue, which includes its Bombshells restaurants.

## Types of clubs

- **Topless vs. fully nude.** Many states and cities limit nudity where alcohol is served, so you'll see topless clubs with full bars and fully nude clubs that are BYOB or juice bars. In Ohio, a club with a liquor permit can stay open past midnight only if it doesn't offer nude entertainment.
- **Upscale gentlemen's clubs:** big rooms, restaurants, bottle service, VIP suites, cover charges.
- **Neighborhood bars:** small stage, cheaper drinks, lower prices.
- **Day shift vs. night shift:** different crowds, different money.
- **Male revues:** usually scheduled shows instead of nightly operations.

## Who works there

| Role | What they do |
|---|---|
| Dancers (entertainers) | Perform on stage and sell private dances and VIP time |
| House mom | Runs the dressing room, schedules, and dancer issues |
| DJ | Music and the stage rotation |
| Floor manager or host | Seats customers, sells VIP, settles disputes |
| Security | Door, ID checks, and watching dancer-customer interactions |
| Bartenders and servers | Drinks, often the club's biggest revenue line |
| Cashier or VIP host | Card payments, dance dollars, VIP bookings |

## How the club makes money

RCI's annual report gives the only detailed public breakdown. In fiscal 2025, its nightclub sales were:

- **43% alcohol**
- **40% service revenue:** cover charges, fees paid by entertainers, and VIP room rentals
- **17% food, merchandise and other**, including ATM and vending income

Other common revenue: bottle service, special events, and surcharges when customers convert card payments into dance dollars.

## How dancers make money

- Stage tips
- Private dances, priced per song
- VIP or champagne room time, usually split with the club

Most clubs have historically treated dancers as independent contractors who get no wage. Instead, dancers often pay the club: a house fee or stage rental per shift, mandatory tip-outs to the DJ, house mom or security, and at some clubs fines for showing up late or missing shifts. In the Philadelphia Penthouse Club case, the federal judge described dancers putting their costume costs, stage rental fee and mandatory tip-outs at risk for the chance to earn several hundred dollars in a six-hour shift.

On a slow night, a dancer can go home with less than they paid to work. That's the core complaint behind most dancer wage suits, and the reason Washington State now caps what clubs can charge.

## The contractor vs. employee fight

- **Courts often side with dancers.** Under federal wage law, courts look at the economic reality of the job, not the contract's label. When the club sets the schedule, prices and rules, dancers are often found to be employees. The Third Circuit upheld a jury verdict of about $4.6 million for Penthouse Club dancers in Philadelphia in 2019. In Hart v. Rick's Cabaret, a New York federal judge awarded dancers about $10.9 million. Déjà Vu-affiliated clubs agreed in 2017 to about $6.5 million covering roughly 28,000 dancers in 18 states.
- **Ohio:** a 2018 federal class action alleged that clubs across the state agreed to pay dancers no wages while charging them fees. In a case against the operator of Sirens in Columbus and related defendants, an $800,000 settlement covering dancers at six Ohio clubs went out for claims in 2026. The settlement notices describe a standardized lease that treated dancers as tenants who paid to work. Details are in [research/ohio.md](../research/ohio.md#key-ohio-cases).
- **California** made dancers employees by default under its ABC test (the Dynamex decision in 2018 and AB 5 in 2019, which didn't exempt exotic dancers). Déjà Vu reclassified its California dancers, and its operations director told The Intercept the cost was so high that four of its clubs shut down. Dancers split: some welcomed wages and protections, others protested the loss of independent-contractor status.
- **RCI** says its entertainers are generally independent contractors. At its Minneapolis Rick's Cabaret and Phoenix Jaguars Club, dancers can choose to be commissioned employees. RCI uses arbitration agreements and lists misclassification as a risk in its annual report.

## Regulation that shapes the business

- **Zoning and licensing:** cities limit where adult businesses can locate, and many license both clubs and entertainers.
- **Liquor control:** the biggest enforcement lever. In the Dayton area, undercover investigations led to a court-ordered shutdown in 2017 and liquor permit revocations in 2018, and Ohio cities routinely object to renewals for problem venues.
- **Conduct rules:** Ohio bans touching between nude or semi-nude dancers and customers.
- **Age limits:** Texas, Florida and Louisiana require dancers to be 21. Washington requires 18, and 21 where alcohol is served.
- **Worker protections:** Washington's 2024 law (security staff, panic buttons, training, fee caps) is the most complete model in the country.
- **Unions:** in May 2023, dancers at Star Garden Topless Dive Bar in North Hollywood voted 17-0 to join Actors' Equity and won recognition, becoming the only unionized strippers in the U.S. at the time.

## Where clubs lose money

- Wage-and-hour litigation: back wages, unjust enrichment awards, legal fees
- Liquor permit loss or a court-ordered nuisance closure
- Violence, and the police calls and objections that follow
- Criminal exposure: when drugs, prostitution or trafficking take hold, owners can go to prison and lose the club (see the Pharaoh's case in [human-trafficking.md](human-trafficking.md#how-it-shows-up-in-clubs))
- Reputation: dancers and customers both have choices

## What well-run clubs do

- Pay dancers fairly, or keep fees low, written, capped and debt-free
- Post prices for dances and VIP time
- Invest in security and keep private areas visible
- Train staff on trafficking, de-escalation and alcohol service
