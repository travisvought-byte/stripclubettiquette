# For dancers

Your rights at work, what fair treatment looks like, and where to get help. This is general information, not legal advice. Laws differ by state, so check the linked source or talk to a lawyer before you act.

## Your rights at a glance

What the law and good practice say, in short. Each point is explained further down this page.

- **Your label doesn't decide your rights.** Many dancers called contractors have been found to be employees. Employees are owed at least minimum wage and their tips. See [Contractor or employee?](#contractor-or-employee)
- **Your tips are yours.** If you're an employee, federal law bars the club from keeping any part of them. See [Your tips are yours](#your-tips-are-yours).
- **No surprise fees.** You should get every fee in writing before your first shift. Washington requires a written contract and bans late fees, no-show fees and debt carried forward. See [House fees, tip-outs and fines](#house-fees-tip-outs-and-fines).
- **No one holds your ID, phone or money.** Not the club, not a manager, not a partner. Someone who does is a warning sign.
- **You can end any dance and leave any time.** A debt doesn't mean you have to stay.
- **A workplace free of harassment and assault.** You can report to the EEOC, and assault is a crime. See [Harassment, assault and safety](#harassment-assault-and-safety).
- **A safe workplace, without payback for speaking up.** Employees can report unsafe conditions to OSHA. See [Health and safety at work](#health-and-safety-at-work).
- **Acting together.** Employees can talk with coworkers about pay, fees and safety, and organize. See [Acting together](#acting-together).
- **Help that doesn't depend on immigration status.** The trafficking hotline helps everyone. See [If someone else is in control](#if-someone-else-is-in-control).

## Contractor or employee?

Most clubs call dancers independent contractors. The label in your contract doesn't decide it.

- Under federal wage law, what counts is the economic reality of the job: who sets the schedule, the prices and the rules, and whether your work is central to the club's business. The Department of Labor says there's no single test and the whole relationship decides it.
- Courts have often found dancers to be employees. The Third Circuit upheld a jury verdict for Penthouse Club dancers in Philadelphia in 2019, and a New York federal judge awarded Rick's Cabaret dancers about $10.9 million. More in [how-strip-clubs-work.md](how-strip-clubs-work.md#the-contractor-vs-employee-fight).
- California treats dancers as employees by default. See [laws-by-state.md](laws-by-state.md#california).

If you're an employee, you're owed at least minimum wage, overtime when it applies, and your tips.

## Your tips are yours

Federal law bars employers from keeping any part of employees' tips, whether directly or through a tip pool. Managers and supervisors can't take a share of a tip pool. A sign or a rule that says otherwise doesn't change the law.

In Washington, clubs must post signs in entertainer areas saying tips don't have to be handed over.

## House fees, tip-outs and fines

Common charges: a house fee or stage rental per shift, required tip-outs to the DJ, house mom or security, and fines for being late or missing a shift.

- If you're an employee, wages have to be paid "free and clear." Fees and kickbacks that push your pay below minimum wage break federal law.
- Washington caps fees at the lesser of $150 or 30% of what you earn outside private areas in any 8-hour period, plus 30% of private-area earnings. Late fees, missed-shift fees, interest and carried-over balances are banned there. See [laws-by-state.md](laws-by-state.md#washington-essb-6105-2024).
- A debt to a club, a manager or a boyfriend that keeps growing, or that you can never pay off, is a warning sign of trafficking. See [human-trafficking.md](human-trafficking.md).

## Keep a record

If something's wrong, a record makes it easier to fix. Keep it somewhere the club can't reach, like your own phone or email.

- Dates and hours you worked
- Every fee, tip-out and fine you paid, and to whom
- Photos of posted fee schedules and house rules
- Texts and messages from managers about schedules, fees or rules
- Any contract or lease you signed

## How to file a wage complaint

- **Federal:** call the Wage and Hour Division at 1-866-487-9243 or file through [dol.gov/whd](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd). It's free, and the law bars your employer from firing or punishing you for filing.
- **Deadlines:** federal wage claims generally must be brought within 2 years, or 3 years if the violation was willful. File sooner rather than later.
- **Ohio:** the Bureau of Wage and Hour Administration takes claims for unpaid minimum wage, overtime, unauthorized deductions and held paychecks. Call 614-644-2239. Filing is free.
- **Class actions:** many dancer cases are brought as class or collective actions. If you get a settlement notice, read the claim deadline. The $800,000 Ohio settlement covering six clubs closed its claims on October 20, 2026.

## Money basics: taxes and savings

General tax information from the IRS, not tax advice. A tax preparer can tell you what applies to you.

- **If you're paid as a contractor, nobody withholds taxes for you.** You owe income tax and self-employment tax. Self-employment tax is 15.3% (12.4% for Social Security and 2.9% for Medicare), and you owe it once your net earnings from self-employment reach $400 for the year.
- **You may need to pay estimated taxes every quarter**, instead of all at once in April. Putting aside part of every night's take for taxes makes that easier, and keeps a slow month from turning into a tax bill you can't pay.
- **Keep your records.** The same records that back up a wage claim (dates, hours, every fee, tip-out and fine) also help at tax time. Keep receipts for work costs and ask a preparer which ones you can deduct.
- **"No tax on tips" may not cover you.** Dancers are on the federal list of tipped jobs for the new deduction for qualified tips, worth up to $25,000 a year for tax years 2025 through 2028, and phasing out above $150,000 in income ($300,000 for joint filers). But the law leaves out tips received in certain fields, including performing arts, and Treasury said it would issue more guidance on that carve-out. Tips also have to be voluntary, so required charges don't count. Ask a preparer before you claim it.

## Harassment, assault and safety

- **Harassment and discrimination:** the EEOC enforces federal law for employees. Call 1-800-669-4000 for information, and file through its online portal. The usual deadline is 180 days, or 300 days where a state or local agency enforces a similar law. If you've been labeled a contractor, ask anyway: the label doesn't decide whether you're covered.
- **Assault:** call 911. Assault is a crime whether it happens on stage, in a private room or in the parking lot.
- **What a safe club looks like:** security on the floor during every open hour, panic buttons in private rooms, restrooms and dressing rooms, a locked dressing room, and a written process for banning violent customers. Washington requires all of these. See the [Club Safety Standard](../standards/club-safety-standard.md).

## Health and safety at work

- **Report unsafe conditions.** If you're an employee, you can file a confidential safety complaint with OSHA online, by phone at 1-800-321-6742, or in person at a local office. OSHA can't issue citations for problems more than six months old, so file soon.
- **If you're punished for speaking up**, file a retaliation complaint with OSHA within 30 days. OSHA's protections are written for employees. If you've been labeled a contractor, ask anyway: as with wage law, the label doesn't decide.
- **Health care without insurance.** Community health centers provide medical and dental care whether or not you have insurance, and charge on a sliding scale based on what you can pay. Find one with HRSA's [Find a Health Center](https://findahealthcenter.hrsa.gov/) tool.
- **Look out for each other.** Buddy systems, customers to watch, rides home, and what to do when a coworker seems controlled: [Looking out for each other](dancer-safety.md).

## Acting together

- Federal labor law protects employees who act together to improve pay and working conditions, with or without a union. That includes talking with coworkers about pay, fees and safety.
- In Washington, dancers organizing as Strippers Are Workers have been meeting since 2018 and pushed for the state's dancer safety laws, including the 2024 law with fee caps, panic buttons and security.
- In May 2023, dancers at Star Garden Topless Dive Bar in North Hollywood voted 17-0 to join Actors' Equity, and the owners agreed to recognize the union.

## If someone else is in control

You may be a victim of trafficking if someone takes your money, holds your ID or phone, threatens you or your family, or says you owe a debt you can never pay off. That's true even if you agreed to the work at first.

- The national hotline is confidential and helps regardless of immigration status: 1-888-373-7888, text 233733.
- Trafficking victims who aren't U.S. citizens may qualify for T nonimmigrant status (a "T visa"), which allows them to stay and work in the U.S.
- Full list of signs and what to do: [human-trafficking.md](human-trafficking.md).
