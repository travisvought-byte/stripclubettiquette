# For club owners and managers

A practical playbook for running a club that keeps its license, stays out of court, holds on to good dancers, and earns a strong rating in this directory.

## Why this matters to your bottom line

- **Wage suits are a constant, expensive risk.** Courts keep finding that dancers are employees. Verdicts and settlements have run from hundreds of thousands of dollars to more than $10 million. In Ohio, an $800,000 settlement covered dancers at six clubs in 2026.
- **Your liquor permit is your business.** Ohio regulators have revoked permits after undercover investigations found drug sales and illegal contact, and cities object to renewals for clubs with heavy police calls.
- **Trafficking can cost you everything.** The owner of Pharaoh's Gentlemen's Club near Buffalo, N.Y., was convicted in 2024 of sex trafficking conspiracy and other crimes tied to the club. He got 25 years, and the club closed.
- **Dancers and customers vote with their feet.** Clubs known for safety, fair fees and honest pricing keep their best entertainers and their regulars.

## The playbook

### 1. Get worker classification right

Talk to employment counsel about your model. Many clubs that called dancers contractors while setting their schedules, prices and house rules have lost in court. The options include employee models, commission models, and contractor arrangements that are genuinely independent. Whatever you choose, put it in writing.

### 2. Make fees fair, written and capped

- One written fee schedule that applies to everyone.
- No late fees, missed-shift fines, interest, or debt carried forward. A dancer should never owe the club money.
- Cap fees. Washington's law is a useful benchmark: per 8-hour period, no more than the lesser of $150 or 30% of the dancer's earnings outside private rooms, plus 30% of private-room earnings.
- Tips belong to the dancer. Disclose any staff tip-outs in writing, or drop them.

### 3. Pay dancers directly

Pay the dancer, never a "manager," boyfriend or driver on the dancer's behalf. A third party collecting a dancer's money is one of the clearest trafficking signs there is.

### 4. Security that protects dancers

- Dedicated security on duty during all open hours, with watching dancer-customer interactions as part of the job.
- Panic buttons in every room where a dancer may be alone with a customer, plus dressing rooms and restrooms. Test them regularly.
- Keypad-locked dressing rooms.
- Written procedures for abusive customers: warning, ejection, banning, and when to call police. Keep a ban list and share it across any clubs you own.
- Escorts to cars on request.

### 5. Keep private areas visible

Hidden rooms invite assaults and illegal activity. After dancers at Atlanta's Cheetah alleged that security guards steered customers in high-priced private rooms to dancers willing to break the club's rules against sexual contact, the club banned tipping security guards and took the doors off its VIP rooms. Cover common areas with cameras, keep staff near private areas, and don't allow locked doors.

### 6. Run a real anti-trafficking program

- Check every entertainer's government ID in person before the first shift, confirm they meet your state's minimum age, and keep records as local law requires. Entertainers hold their own IDs.
- Train every employee (managers, DJs, house moms, security, bartenders, servers) at hire and every year on trafficking indicators and how to report. Washington requires this.
- Post the national hotline (1-888-373-7888, text 233733) and your state hotline in dressing rooms and restrooms, in English and Spanish. Ohio encourages adult entertainment establishments to display the state poster.
- Name one manager responsible for trafficking concerns, and write down the escalation steps.
- Zero tolerance for prostitution and for staff who steer customers to "extras."
- Never retaliate against anyone who raises a concern.
- Get to know your local anti-trafficking coalition or victim-services group. Club owners' groups have run trainings with federal agents since around 2010.

The indicators are in [human-trafficking.md](human-trafficking.md#signs-to-watch-for).

### 7. Honest customer pricing

Post prices for dances, VIP time and drinks, plus any dance-dollar surcharge or ATM fee. Give itemized receipts, and train staff to confirm card charges with the customer. Billing disputes turn into chargebacks, bad reviews and fraud complaints.

### 8. Responsible alcohol service

Train every server and bartender in responsible service. It cuts violence and liability. Some states tie certification to legal protection: RCI says its Texas clubs require servers to be certified under a state-approved program, which protects against dram shop claims.

### 9. Listen to your dancers

Give dancers a way to report problems that doesn't run through the person they're reporting, and give written reasons when you let someone go.

## How this directory rates you, and how to improve your listing

- Ratings come from public records: court cases, permit actions, criminal cases, and your own published policies. See [METHODOLOGY.md](../METHODOLOGY.md).
- **Correct your listing:** send updates with a source.
- **Respond to a record:** submit a short written response, and we'll link it next to the record.
- **Show your work:** publish your fee schedule, training records and safety measures. Positive records are logged and displayed.
- **Meet the standard:** see the [Club Safety Standard](../standards/club-safety-standard.md).
- Ratings are never for sale.
