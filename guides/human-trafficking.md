# Human trafficking and strip clubs

What trafficking is, how it shows up in clubs, the signs to watch for, and what to do about it. For customers, club staff, dancers and advocates.

## Get help or report

| Who | How |
|---|---|
| **Emergency** | 911 |
| **National Human Trafficking Hotline** | Call 1-888-373-7888, text 233733, or chat at [humantraffickinghotline.org/chat](https://humantraffickinghotline.org/chat). TTY 711. Free, confidential, 24/7, in 200+ languages. Run by Polaris, a nonprofit. It is not law enforcement or immigration. |
| **Ohio Human Trafficking Hotline** | 844-END-OHHT (844-363-6448), text ENDOHHT to 847411, or use the END OHHT app. Run 24/7 by the Ohio Bureau of Criminal Investigation. Tips go straight to law enforcement. Non-emergency. |
| **Missing or exploited children** | National Center for Missing & Exploited Children: 1-800-THE-LOST (1-800-843-5678) |
| **Federal law enforcement tip line** | DHS Homeland Security Investigations: 1-866-347-2423 |

**Which number?** If you're unsure, or the person might fear police or immigration consequences, start with the national hotline. Its advocates connect people to local services and pass tips to authorities in some cases. Hotline staff are mandated reporters, so some information (such as suspected abuse of a minor) may be shared with authorities. If you want police involved now, use the Ohio hotline or 911.

## What trafficking is

Under federal law, sex trafficking means getting an adult to perform a commercial sex act through force, fraud or coercion, or involving anyone under 18 in commercial sex, with or without force, fraud or coercion. A "commercial sex act" is any sex act where anything of value is given or received by anyone.

Force, fraud and coercion can look like violence or threats, lies about the job or the pay, debt that can never be paid off, taking someone's ID, threats against family, isolation, or controlling someone through a drug addiction.

## Dancing is not trafficking

Most dancers are adults who chose the work. Treating every dancer as a victim is wrong and it backfires: it pushes dancers away from the people and services that could help when something does go wrong, and it buries real victims in noise.

Trafficking is about control. The question isn't "is this person dancing?" It's "is someone else controlling them, or are they under 18?"

## How it shows up in clubs

- **The trafficker is often a boyfriend.** Polaris analyzed thousands of hotline cases and found that in strip clubs, traffickers tend to be less networked than in cantina-based trafficking and are often the victim's intimate partner. Victims are mostly U.S. citizen women and girls, with some from Eastern Europe. Male victims have been reported too.
- **Control through debt and violence.** Polaris found victims in this category are often trapped by debts they can't repay, and many face beatings, sexual assault and threats against their families.
- **Sometimes it's the club itself.** In December 2024 a federal jury convicted the owner of Pharaoh's Gentlemen's Club, outside Buffalo, N.Y., of sex trafficking conspiracy, bribing a DEA agent, maintaining a drug-involved premises, witness tampering and drug crimes. Prosecutors said drugs and coercion were used to force dancers into sex acts inside the club. In May 2026 he was sentenced to 25 years. The club closed.
- **"Extras" in private rooms.** A club that tolerates sex for money in VIP rooms creates the market traffickers exploit.

## Signs to watch for

No single sign proves trafficking. Several together are worth a call.

**Control**
- Someone else holds the dancer's ID, money or phone, or collects their earnings.
- The same person drops the dancer off and picks them up, and seems to be in charge.
- Someone answers for them, or their answers sound scripted.
- They mention a quota they have to hit or a debt they have to work off.
- They don't know where they're staying, or someone else moves them from city to city.

**Wellbeing**
- Fear, anxiety, or acting like they're being watched.
- Injuries, signs of abuse, or tattoos that look like branding (a name, "property of," money symbols).
- Someone seems to be supplying their drugs and using that to control them.

**Age**
- They look or act younger than 18, or their ID doesn't seem to match them.

**The club**
- Staff steering customers to private rooms for "extras."
- Dancers recruited through "managers" or "agents" who take a cut.
- Dancers who can't leave when they want, or who owe the club money.

## What to do

**Customers**
- Don't confront anyone or try a rescue. It can put the person in more danger.
- Write down what you saw: date, time, place, descriptions, names used, license plates.
- Call or text the hotline. Call 911 if someone is in immediate danger.
- Don't buy sex. It's illegal, and it's the demand traffickers profit from.

**Club staff**
- Tell your designated manager and follow the club's procedure. If there isn't one, call the hotline yourself.
- Don't tip off the suspected trafficker.

**Dancers**
- If someone takes your money, holds your ID, threatens you or your family, or says you owe a debt you can never pay off, that may be trafficking, even if you agreed to the work at first.
- A debt to a club, a manager or a boyfriend is not a reason you have to stay.
- The national hotline is confidential and helps regardless of immigration status. You can call just to talk through your options.

## What clubs should do

The short list: check every entertainer's ID in person, pay dancers directly, train all staff every year, post the hotlines in dressing rooms and restrooms, keep private areas visible, enforce zero tolerance for "extras," and never retaliate against anyone who reports. The full playbook is in [for-club-operators.md](for-club-operators.md), and the checklist is in the [Club Safety Standard](../standards/club-safety-standard.md).

## Advocacy: what helps

- **Worker protections that reduce vulnerability.** Washington's 2024 law requires staff training on identifying and reporting trafficking, panic buttons, dedicated security, bans for customers credibly accused of trafficking or violence, and caps on the fees dancers pay. Low fees and no debt take away a key tool of control.
- **Posters and training.** Many states require hotline posters in adult businesses. Ohio's law encourages, but doesn't require, adult entertainment establishments to display the state poster. Training only works when staff know exactly what to do next.
- **Industry partnerships.** Since around 2010, club owners' groups have hosted trafficking trainings with federal agents for owners, dancers, bartenders and bouncers. Critics have said suspected victims identified this way are sometimes mishandled by law enforcement, which is one reason a services-first hotline matters.
- **Age-21 laws.** Texas, Florida and Louisiana raised the minimum age for dancers to 21, citing trafficking, and federal appeals courts have upheld those laws. Dancers and clubs who challenged them argued they restrict lawful work and expression, and Florida's challengers argued the state hadn't shown a link between clubs and trafficking. Details in [laws-by-state.md](laws-by-state.md#the-age-21-debate).
